Agents that do real engineering work

What happened when we stopped treating AI as autocomplete and started treating it as an engineering team — with specs, tests, and supervision.

July 2026

Every company I talk to is somewhere on the same curve with AI and engineering. First it's autocomplete. Then it's "we let developers use a chatbot." Then someone asks the uncomfortable question: if this thing can write code, review code, and reason about systems — why is it still sitting in a side window while we do the work the old way?

Over the past year I've been living on the far side of that question. Not in a lab, and not with a vendor's demo — inside a real company, against real production systems, with an engineering estate that includes a two-decade-old order engine, a payments stack, and the accumulated decisions of eighteen years. What I found changed how I think about engineering capacity entirely: AI agents can do real engineering work — if you build the operating model around them the way you'd build it around an engineering team.

What "real work" means

I don't mean generating boilerplate. Over a period of months, working this way, we rebuilt a legacy .NET order engine as a modern Python service — starting with line-level behavioral specifications reverse-engineered from the original, and ending with the new system validated live against every external integration it touches: payments, tax, fraud screening, fulfillment, provisioning. In one validation pass, the new system reproduced the legacy system's most intricate encoding behavior against real production records, and surfaced real bugs that code review alone had missed.

The same practice migrated a two-hundred-table data warehouse between cloud platforms — every table validated for row counts, column parity, and content checksums, which caught a data-fidelity defect that row counts alone would have waved through. It audited and cleaned up years of accumulated cloud infrastructure, cutting monthly spend substantially while closing disaster-recovery gaps rather than creating them. It root-caused a production incident down to a single subtle line of schema-resolution logic.

None of that is autocomplete. That's the work of an engineering team — specification, implementation, validation, operations, forensics.

The part everyone skips

Here's what I tell other executives: the model is the easy part. What makes this work is everything around it — the operating model. Ours has a few load-bearing pieces:

Written standards. Agents follow the engineering culture you document, not the one you wish you had. We maintain explicit working standards — research before acting, smallest change that solves the problem, tests with every change, no silent assumptions — and every agent session starts from them. It forced us to write down things we'd left implicit for years, which turned out to be valuable for the humans too.

Governed access. Agents need to touch real systems to do real work — and that access has to be deliberate. We built a shared platform layer (using MCP, the emerging standard for tool access) that gives agents governed, auditable access to enterprise systems: the same interface across AI clients, with authentication, scoping, and an audit trail. The agent that updates a ticket and the agent that queries a database go through the same controlled doorway.

Validation as the center of gravity. The discipline shifts from writing the work to proving the work. Read-only credentials by default. Production writes gated behind explicit human approval. Test environments where the blast radius is zero. Golden-record comparisons against the legacy system's actual behavior. When an agent claims something works, the claim gets exercised end-to-end against real integrations before anyone believes it.

Supervision with real authority. A human — in our case, often me — reviews the plan before execution, approves anything irreversible, and owns the outcome. "Under supervision" isn't a disclaimer; it's the job. The skill it demands looks less like typing and more like running a very fast, very literal engineering team: clear specifications, sharp review, and knowing where the bodies are buried in your own systems.

Why executives should care

The economics are hard to ignore: work that would traditionally consume several teams' quarters happened with one supervising engineer and an agent practice. But the deeper shift is strategic. For twenty years, engineering capacity was the binding constraint on every roadmap I owned. That constraint is moving — and the companies that figure out the operating model first get the compounding advantage.

The failure mode is equally clear. Half the companies out there have responded to AI by rebranding an existing role and buying a platform. Adoption without an operating model produces either nothing (pilots that never touch production) or chaos (ungoverned agents with write access). The path that works is the boring one: build real systems, prove them, write down the rules, and scale what survives contact with production.

That's what I mean when I say AI transformation isn't bought — it's built. And the leaders who build it themselves are the ones who can govern it credibly.
