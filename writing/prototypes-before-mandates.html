Prototypes before mandates: how AI adoption actually spreads

Company-wide AI adoption didn't start with a policy. It started with working software. The arc from personal prototypes to enterprise mandate.

July 2026

Roughly half the companies out there have responded to AI by renaming an executive role and issuing a policy. I've watched how that goes: an "AI strategy" everyone nods at, a tool nobody quite trusts, and twelve months later an inventory document instead of a transformation.

Our company-wide AI adoption took a different route, and the sequence is the point: it started with working software, not with authority.

The arc

First, I retrained. In late 2023 I went back to school — MIT's data science and machine learning program — because I refused to lead a transformation I couldn't personally evaluate. Rigorous months, real projects. It reset what I believed was possible.

Then I built, before asking anyone's permission. A retrieval-augmented chat application answering from real content. A conversational agent inside our messaging platform that could search and act on our project tracker in plain English. A shared platform giving AI agents governed access to enterprise systems. An agentic engineering practice that rebuilt a legacy platform and migrated a two-hundred-table warehouse. Working systems, run against real problems, with results you could inspect.

Then came the presentation. When I finally made the executive case for company-wide adoption, I wasn't projecting what AI might do for us. I was demonstrating what it had already done — here, on our systems, with our data, by our standards. The difference in the room between a vendor's promise and a colleague's working system is hard to overstate. The case made itself; several departments moved fast on their own.

Only then, the mandate. Adoption became my formal remit: strategy, department-by-department rollout, and the guardrails that keep it safe. The authority came after the proof — which is exactly why it worked. A mandate without demonstrated substance gets compliance. A mandate built on working systems gets believers.

What I'd tell another executive team

Demos beat decks — build first, even if small. One working system that solves a real internal pain converts more people than any strategy document. Pick a visible, annoying problem and make it disappear.

Someone senior has to get their hands dirty. Not every executive needs to write code. But if nobody at the leadership table has personally built with this technology, every decision is delegated faith — in vendors, in hype, in whoever speaks most confidently. Depth at the top is what lets you tell the two apart.

Guardrails accelerate adoption; they don't slow it. Departments hesitated until there were clear rules: what agents may touch, what needs approval, what gets audited. The guardrails were what made saying yes safe. Governance designed by someone who builds reads as enablement; governance designed by someone who doesn't reads as fear.

Expect uneven speed — and let the eager run. Some departments sprint, others watch. That's healthy. The sprinters become your internal case studies, and the pattern spreads on results, not pressure.

The through-line: AI transformation isn't bought — it's built. Built first in small working systems, then in the confidence of the people around them, and only last in the org chart.
